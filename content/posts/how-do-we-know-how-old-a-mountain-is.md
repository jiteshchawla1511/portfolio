## 1. How Do We Know?

The Himalayan range is young in terms of geological scale, but what if I told you that some parts of it are even older than Mt. Everest itself? Sounds bizarre, right? 

So what does it actually mean when we read or hear that something is *“X million years old”*? How can geologists be so precise about it? There was no spectator to watch or record things for us, since human civilization is itself very new and these geological events happened so long before us. 

So how can someone know about all the gargantuan geography around us—these mountains, valleys, plateaus, and beaches? How can someone so newly arrived decipher these ancient wonders? 

Well, that is what we are going to explore in this blog series, where I will try my best to explain how we can understand these geological marvels.

The other day, I was reading Sherlock Holmes and noticed a striking similarity between a detective and a geologist. Not that their playing field is the same, but the way they think and approach a problem is remarkably alike. Detectives don't arrive at a crime scene expecting everything to be laid out in a clean, linear sequence. They look for clues, connect seemingly unrelated pieces of evidence, eliminate possibilities, and slowly reconstruct what might have happened.

The same is true for a geologist. For a detective, it might be blood samples or fingerprints; for a geologist, the clues are:

- **Rocks** → tell us what materials existed and how they formed
- **Rock layers** → tell us about the sequence of events
- **Fossils** → tell us about ancient life and environments
- **Faults and folds** → show that rocks were squeezed, stretched, or moved
- **Sediments** → reveal erosion and what was happening in surrounding mountains
- **Volcanic layers** → provide useful time markers
- **Isotopes** → act as natural geological clocks

And just like a detective, a geologist rarely solves the case with a single clue. The real story emerges when several independent pieces of evidence start pointing in the same direction.

## 2. A Mountain Doesn't Have One Birthday

A mountain does not have just one birthday. So what does that actually mean? We often hear statements like *“this mountain is 50 million years old.”* 

**What exactly does that 50 million years refer to?**

Because there are several different “ages” we could be talking about:

| What we're dating | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Rock age** | When that particular rock or mineral formed |
| **Deformation age** | When rocks were squeezed, folded, or faulted |
| **Mountain-building age** | When tectonic processes began creating the mountain system |
| **Uplift / exhumation age** | When rocks moved toward the surface |
| **Landscape age** | When the present-looking hills, valleys, and slopes developed |

Let's understand this with an analogy. Suppose we have thousands of bricks that are 500 years old, and we build a brand-new house using them. Ideally, the house is brand new, but the bricks used inside it are 500 years old! 

The same relationship exists between a mountain range and the rocks that make it up. A mountain is not a ready-made item that just popped into existence out of nowhere—it is the culmination of a long series of geological events.

Rocks can form, get buried deep underground, experience immense pressure and temperature, get squeezed and folded by tectonic forces, and eventually be pushed back toward the surface. Then erosion starts shaping them, slowly carving out the mountains, valleys, and landscapes that we see today.

So when we say that a mountain is “X million years old,” we need to be careful about **which part of that story we're actually talking about.**

And this is where things get interesting: **how do geologists figure out the order in which all these events happened in the first place?**

---

## 3. But How Do We Know What Happened First?

Now the question arises: how do geologists determine what happened first?

Long story short: you cannot always determine the exact numerical age of an event just by looking at the rocks. But you can often figure out which event happened before which. This is where one of the oldest ideas in geology comes in: **Relative Dating**.

As the name suggests, we determine the relative ordering of geological events. It doesn't give you an exact number in years, but it establishes the sequence.

If one rock layer lies above another, if one rock cuts through another, or if pieces of an older rock are found trapped inside a younger one, these relationships give geologists direct clues about what came first and what happened later. And that is a remarkably powerful starting point when you're trying to reconstruct a history that nobody was around to witness.

---

## 4. Okay, But How Do We Know the Actual Age?

So, relative dating tells us what happened first and what happened later. But there is still one big question left:

**How do we actually know the number?**

How can a geologist look at a rock and say, *“This rock formed 500 million years ago”*?

This is where **Radiometric Dating** comes into the picture.

Some elements found in minerals are naturally unstable. Their atoms slowly transform into other elements over time through a process known as **radioactive decay**.

Now here comes the fascinating part: we cannot predict exactly when one particular atom will decay. But when we have a huge number of these atoms, their rate of decay follows a very predictable statistical pattern.

And this predictable pattern gives us something incredibly useful — **a natural clock ticking inside the rock.**

This is where the concept of **half-life** comes in.

A half-life is simply the amount of time it takes for half of the radioactive parent atoms in a sample to decay into stable daughter atoms.

Imagine we start with 1,000 radioactive atoms whose half-life is 100 million years:

- After **100 million years** → ~500 remain
- After another **100 million years** (200 Ma total) → ~250 remain
- After another **100 million years** (300 Ma total) → ~125 remain
- And so on.

If we know the original radioactive isotope and its decay rate, we can measure how much parent material remains compared to how much daughter product has accumulated. From this ratio, scientists can calculate when that mineral formed or when a specific geological process locked those atoms in place.

And that is how we move from simply saying:

> *“This happened before that.”*

to being able to say:

> *“This happened approximately 500 million years ago.”*

But remember the catch: **we are dating the rock or mineral — not automatically the entire mountain.**

---

## 5. One Date Doesn't Tell the Whole Story

So now we know that radiometric dating can give us a number. Sounds straightforward, right?

Well, **not quite.**

Remember what we discussed earlier: a mountain doesn't have just one birthday.

Suppose a geologist finds a rock in the Himalayas and dates one of its zircon minerals to **500 million years old**. Does that mean the Himalayas as a mountain range are 500 million years old?

**No.**

It means that particular mineral, or the magmatic event that formed it, dates back roughly 500 million years. The actual tectonic collision that pushed the Himalayas skyward happened much later (around 50 million years ago).

This is why geologists don't simply pick up one rock, run one laboratory test, and call it a day. They collect different lines of evidence and see how they piece together:

- One rock might reveal when it originally crystallized.
- Another mineral might record when it was metamorphosed and deformed under pressure.
- Thermochronology techniques can tell us when the rock cooled as it was pushed closer to the surface.
- Sediments deposited in surrounding basins reveal when erosion accelerated.
- Marine fossils high up on mountain peaks tell us about ancient oceanic environments before the collision.

Slowly, these individual clues come together. 

**A detective rarely solves a case from one clue. A geological history is no different.**

The real story emerges when different pieces of evidence start converging on the same sequence of events. Rather than searching for **one single age**, geologists reconstruct **the complete biography** of the mountain.

## 6. So, Can We Really Know Earth's Past?

So after all of this, can we truly know what happened millions or even billions of years before us?

We obviously weren't there. There was no camera recording the Himalayas rising. No one was standing beside the ancient Aravallis when they were higher than the Alps. There was no one around to witness the colossal volcanic eruptions that poured out the Deccan Traps.

And yet, we can reconstruct these stories.

Not because we have a magic machine, but because **the Earth was kind enough to leave clues behind.**

Rocks tell us what formed and what conditions they endured. Rock layers tell us about the order of events. Fossils tell us about ancient life and ecosystems. Faults and folds record tectonic collisions. Sediments document erosion and changing landscapes. And radioactive isotopes give us clocks to calibrate the timeline.

None of these clues tells the whole story on its own. But woven together, they allow us to assemble a vivid picture of Earth's deep past.

**The sheer possibility of understanding the history and lifecycle of the landscapes and mountains that I love.**

We look at something that seems eternal and permanent — a mountain peak, a gorge, a plateau — but what we are really looking at is **a single snapshot in an epic, ongoing story.**
